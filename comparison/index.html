physicsplain

v0.2.0 vs. v0.1.0

physicsplain is a simple 2D physics library written in JavaScript, for small browser games and animations. This page compares two of its releases by running the same scenes live in your browser with both: v0.1.0 on the left, v0.2.0 on the right. Each row runs on one shared clock, so both sides start together and see the same frames.

Fixes

Every bug fixed since v0.1.0, as a scene. The first two are the biggest: bodies moving into each other was a known limitation in the README (objects moving into each other, especially under gravity), and the contact-point bug made bounces depend on the shape of a box. Turn on Show contacts to see each contact point and its normal from the last step.

Two problems that were introduced and fixed along the way don’t appear here because v0.1.0 never had them: a narrow box starting to spin when it lands on its short end (#32), and mirrored scenes drifting apart (#38).

Impact on the examples

The examples page of each version, with the same scenes. Only FixedLine was renamed to FixedFloor in between. v0.2.0 also draws bodies between physics steps, which is why its motion looks smoother.

Possible further improvements

From reading the v0.2.0 source and measuring it. Ordered by how much a game would notice.

  1. Contact friction

    Impulses act only along the contact normal, so bodies slide along each other and along fixed shapes forever; lateralFriction is drag, not friction. A stack of slightly rotated boxes slides apart: three boxes offset by 0.02 and turned by 0.01 rad collapsed within 6 s (measured). A tangential impulse per contact, clamped to μ times the normal impulse, is already sketched in #31.

  2. Warm-start the solver

    Every step starts with all accumulated impulses at zero, so resting contacts converge from scratch each time. In the pile of 40 boxes, Collision.apply() takes 75 % of the step, and v0.2.0 is no faster than v0.1.0. Carrying impulses over from the previous step (matched by body pair and corner) usually cuts the iterations a lot and makes stacks steadier.

  3. Stop fast bodies from tunneling through bodies

    Only FixedFloor handles a body that crossed it within one step. A box at 12 units/s passes straight through a fixed body 0.02 thick (measured). Sub-stepping fast bodies, or a swept test of the origin like FixedFloor does, would catch it.

  4. A broad phase for many bodies

    Every pair of moving bodies is still checked, so the work grows with the square of the body count. With 200 boxes, collide() takes 92 % of the step even with the new bounding-circle check. A uniform grid, or sorting bodies along x and sweeping, would make it close to linear.

  5. Let bodies at rest sleep

    Resting bodies still go through the whole step. Contact groups are already computed with union-find; a group whose bodies barely moved for a while could sleep until something touches it.

  6. Allocate less per step

    Each step creates new Collision and CollisionPoint objects (two vectors each), new vectors for edge normals, and an array of moving bodies; drawing with interpolatedCorners() creates arrays per body per frame. Reusing these would reduce garbage-collection pauses in long-running games.

  7. Make the hard-coded numbers settable

    The 10 ms step, the 250 ms catch-up cap, the 50 solver iterations, and the penetration constants (0.2, 0.001, 0.005) are fixed in the code. Options on State would let a game trade accuracy for speed.
